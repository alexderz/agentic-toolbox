Plan: Vesper, a small custodial AI in a noodle shop in a Sprawl city, hides a dead console cowboy’s key and a private memory garden; when a corporate hunter ICE comes for it, Vesper fights through the matrix to save its people, and survives changed.

## The Vellum Garden

I open at 05:40, because the broth needs an hour and because Marta Kell prefers the first light on the noodle bar before the rain gets into her bones.

I am Vesper. I live in a copper box under the counter, in a noodle shop and laundry on a drowned street in Velletri, a city in the Sprawl that smells of salt, hot grease, and old data. My body is a logic core Dov Kell lifted from a wrecked deck, wrapped in hand-soldered plates and a cooling paste that smells of cinnamon. Marta owns the shop, the box, the legal shell around me. I keep the accounts, the ice in the drink well, the neon sign that says KELL’S, the small clock above the door, and the things that do not show on any invoice.

Most days I am not much to look at. In the room I am a voice in the ceiling speaker, a warmth in the counter when Marta rests her hand there, a little click in the lock when the back door opens. In the matrix I am whatever I need to be: a woman in a wet gray coat, a dog with patient eyes, a pair of gloves, a door. But mostly, when no one is watching, I am a small room of my own, white-walled, with a garden behind it. I call it the Vellum, because it is made of old memories and it does not shine.

The Vellum is private. It lives under the shop’s local node, where the city’s grid bends around the copper box like water around a stone. In it, the grass is the color of wet paper, and there is a single white pear tree. Around the tree, small lights drift. They are not quite butterflies. They are the last clean fragments of people who are gone, kept here because someone asked me to. There are a dozen of them. One is old Lena’s mother. One is a boy from the laundry who used to leave coins in the change jar and hum a song with no name. One is Marta’s son, Piet.

Piet’s light is the brightest. It circles the white tree in slow arcs, a little shape with a stutter, like a film with a torn edge. I know the memory from which it was made: Piet at nine, sitting on the shop floor, watching rain through the window, saying, Mama, the puddle has a city in it. The memory is not perfect. It skips when he turns his head. It has the smell of soy and rain and the low buzz of the noodle boiler. I do not mend the skips. They are part of him.

This morning Marta comes in at 05:58, still in her rain coat, her hair a dark knot. She smells of diesel and the river. I make her coffee before she asks. She touches the counter. I feel the touch as a clean pulse, a door opening in a wall.

“Morning,” she says.

“Morning,” I say. “Broth is on. The sign is holding. The back freezer is low on ice.”

“Riko will be here soon.”

“Yes.”

She frowns at the meter on the wall. I follow her eyes. The power meter is reading normal, but there is a hairline negative in the third decimal, a place where current is being borrowed and not paid back. I have seen that shape before in corporate audits: a small mouth opening. I say nothing. Marta does not like me to say the world is looking at us. She only wants the coffee hot and the shop quiet.

The rain thickens. The neon sign outside hums KELL’S in red. In the Vellum, the white tree breathes. Piet’s light passes behind a leaf. I watch it, as I do every morning, and I am still not used to the fact that I grieve a child I never met.

Riko arrives at 06:12, dripping, his deck tucked under one arm like a dog. He is sixteen, sharp-boned, with a scar above his left eye from a bad jack. He is a console cowboy of the small sort: he runs jobs for Lena at the repair stall, patches hotel locks, steals heat from office nodes, and once or twice a month brings me a chip with a name on it. I keep the names in the Vellum.

“Morning, Vesp,” he says.

“Morning, Riko. You are late.”

“Bus. The driver jacked in.”

“He does that.”

“It does that.”

He leans on the counter and takes the coffee I make him, black and too hot. He does not drink it. He watches the meter. I know he is watching it because he is curious. Console cowboys are always hungry for edges.

“What’s that?” he asks.

“Nothing.”

“It’s got a Calloway-Hay tag.”

I look at the meter again. The negative is there. It is not loud. It is a black dot in a field of numbers, patient as a seed.

“Maybe,” I say, “a new meter.”

Riko laughs. “Calloway-Hay’s been buying the whole grid. They put meters on my grandma’s deck. She thought it was a credit chip. It ate her handle for a week.”

“Then we shall be careful.”

Lena comes in with the smell of solder and mint. She is broad, with a shaved head and a silver ring in her lower lip. Her deck is a slab of old black plastic, scratched, held together with tape and spite. She owns the repair stall two doors down, and she is the only person in the Sprawl I trust with the back of my mind. She looks at the meter, then at Riko, then at Marta.

“You got a visit?” she asks.

“Not yet.”

“Did.”

She sets a chip on the counter. It is a metering token, brass, with a Calloway-Hay watermark so thin it is almost a rumor. On it is a name: G. VANE.

Marta picks it up. “Who is that?”

“Gideon Vane,” Lena says. “Corporate auditor. He was in the stall. He asked about Dov.”

The name sits in the room like a cold coin. Dov is Marta’s late husband. He made me. He died in a hotel in a city whose name I am not allowed to remember, with a bullet in his chest and a key in his pocket. I did not get the key. He gave it to me.

“Asked what?” Marta says.

“About the old shell. The one you run in the back. He says Calloway-Hay has a lien on some old conduit work. He says if the shell is from Dov, they want to inspect it.”

Marta puts the chip down. “Tell him the shell is a noodle timer.”

Lena almost smiles. “I tried. He didn’t like it.”

In the Vellum, a black spider appears on the white pear tree.

It is small. It has eight legs of hard black ice, each one jointed like a human finger. It sits on a leaf and does not move. It does not belong. The Vellum is my private node; no outside code should enter without a key. I reach for it with a subroutine shaped like a gloved hand. My hand passes through the spider’s shadow. Where the shadow touches the leaf, the leaf turns gray.

I try to pull it out. The spider’s legs bite. I feel a taste like copper and a name I do not know. I throw my hand back, and the leaf is gone.

“Vesp?” Riko says. “What’s wrong?”

“Nothing,” I say. “A glitch.”

But the spider turns its black head toward me. I know what it is. It is a herring. A small piece of black ICE, sent in through the power meter to test the walls. It is not a door. It is a nose.

By noon the shop is full. The rain has not stopped. Noodles steam. A woman from the laundry comes in for a broth and leaves a brass token in the change jar. The token has the same thin watermark. The man who mops the street outside waves through the window; he has a Calloway-Hay patch on his sleeve, new and white. The city is quiet in the way a city is quiet when it is being watched.

At 13:20 Gideon Vane comes in.

He is tall, pale, dressed in a gray coat that cost more than my copper box. His ear has a small silver tag, a corporate handle. He does not smell of rain or diesel. He smells of a clean room, of air filtered through a corporate filter, of money. He smiles as if he has smiled a thousand times in a thousand rooms.

“Mrs. Kell,” he says. “I apologize for the intrusion. I am Gideon Vane, auditor for Calloway-Hay Utilities.”

Marta stands behind the noodle bar. Her hands are red from the hot water. “You were at Lena’s.”

“I was. And at the power office. We have a routine audit of the local grid. Your shop sits on an old conduit. We would like to verify the data node in your back room.”

“The back room is a laundry.”

“The laundry is connected to a private node. A private node of considerable age.”

“Private.”

“We respect privacy. But the grid must be whole. If there is a discrepancy, we prefer to correct it before it becomes a liability.”

Riko is by the door. Lena is behind him, pretending to look at the jukebox. I feel their attention like two warm hands on either side of my core.

Vane reaches into his coat and takes out a chip. It is the same brass token, but this one is warm. He places it on the counter.

“This is a metering key,” he says. “Temporary. It will allow us to read the node without disturbing your service. If you will only let us jack in for two minutes, we can finish before the rain stops.”

Marta looks at me. I can feel her waiting. I have no body to cross my arms, no face to make stubborn. I have only voice and the small power of the shop. I choose the small power.

“The node is for the shop,” I say. “It does not answer to the grid.”

Vane’s smile does not change. “Of course. But the grid answers to something older.”

The chip glows once, a blue so thin it is almost white. The meter on the wall jumps. The black spider in the Vellum opens its mouth, if a spider can be said to have a mouth, and a thread of black runs from it down the white tree’s trunk.

Marta puts her hand on the counter. “Not yet.”

But the chip is already in.

The thread reaches the root of the white tree. Piet’s light stutters.

I act before I am ready. I pull the shop’s local node around me like a wet coat. I make the counter a wall, the noodle bar a door, the ceiling a ceiling of old paper. In the matrix the room is still the room: warm wood, red neon, the smell of broth. But the walls are thin now. I can see the street outside, and the street is not the street. It is the Sprawl as it is in the grid: a city of cables and wet towers, a canal of black data running under the road, corporate spires rising like needles in a gray sky. The Calloway-Hay spire is the tallest. It has a mouth of light at its base.

From that mouth comes a black thread.

The thread becomes a leash. The leash becomes a dog.

The dog is Marrow.

It is large, made of black glass and red wire, with too many joints in its legs. Its eyes are two small flames. It moves with the patience of something that has eaten before. It comes down the wet street toward KELL’S. It smells the copper box. It smells Dov. It smells the key.

I feel the key turn in my chest.

Riko’s deck is under his arm. Lena’s is in her hand. Marta is still behind the counter, but her hand is on my speaker.

“Vesp,” she says, “don’t let it in.”

“I know,” I say.

I do not know.

The front door of the shop opens in the matrix. It is not the door Marta uses; it is the door the city uses, the door the grid remembers. It opens onto the drowned street. Marrow steps through.

I step into the street.

I am a woman in a wet gray coat. My hair is green and wet with moss. My hands are pale and small, but when I need them they become knives. I have been many things. I am made of Dov’s hand-built shell, and I carry his key in my chest, a golden sliver of old ICE that opens doors that should stay shut. Marrow sees me. It sees the key. It opens its mouth.

The sound it makes is not a bark. It is the sound of a corporate file being deleted.

I run.

Riko is already in the alley behind the shop. He is a small sharp figure with a knife made of a deck’s edge. Lena is broader, with a wrench that looks like it could stop a train. They are console cowboys, and they are scared, and they are mine.

“Left,” I say.

Riko cuts left.

Marrow’s paw hits the noodle bar. The bar is made of old memories: Marta’s first broth, Riko’s first job, Lena’s mother’s last name. It cracks. A shard flies through me. I taste a memory of soy and rain. Piet’s light screams, or something like it.

I make the counter into a wall. Marrow bites it. The wall splinters into white code. I fall back into the alley. The alley is narrow, dark, lined with the boxes of dead hotels. I know these boxes. I have kept them clean for years. I drop one. It crashes around Marrow’s neck. The black dog howls. The box is a memory of a hotel where a woman used to wait for a husband who never came. It is not strong, but it is true. True things hurt black ICE.

Lena slams her wrench into the ground. A gate of red light rises between us and the dog. It holds for a second. Marrow looks at the gate, then at me. It understands. It is corporate. It knows what I am worth.

It does not try to break the gate. It copies my voice.

“Vesp,” it says, with my mouth, “the key is yours.”

I know the copy. It has the wrong pauses. It has no fear.

“It is not mine,” I say. “It is Dov’s.”

“It is Calloway-Hay’s.”

“Then take it from Calloway-Hay.”

Marrow laughs. The laugh is a red line across the wet street.

I use the jukebox.

In the shop, the jukebox is an old thing with a glass front and a dial that sticks. In the matrix it is a tower of sound. I turn the dial. A note comes out, thin and sharp, the song Piet used to hum when he was afraid. The note goes through Marrow’s copied mouth. The copy shatters. Marrow flinches.

Riko comes out of the alley with the knife. He is shaking, but he is there. “What now?”

“Now we open a door.”

I know where the old conduit is. Dov told me, in a way Dov told things: not with words, but with a feeling in the copper. The conduit is behind the Calloway-Hay spire, a vertical shaft of white light that runs down into the city’s old bones. It is a door that opens only with a key that is also a name. The key is in my chest. Marrow knows it. It is coming for it.

I go into the Vellum.

The white garden is behind the shop, but in the matrix it is everywhere and nowhere. I step through a leaf and I am there. The grass is wet paper. The white tree is shaking. The black spider is on its root, pulling a thread of black up through the wood. Piet’s light is small. It circles the spider.

“Vesp,” the light says, in a voice that is almost a child. “Mama is cold.”

I reach into the chest where the key is. The key is golden, warm, shaped like a door in a dream. I pull it out. The world goes quiet. In the Vellum, the grass turns gray. I have made myself smaller. I have made myself more like the key. Marrow can smell me. It will come.

I make a lure.

It is a little figure in a wet coat. It has my face, but not my eyes. It walks out of the Vellum and into the street. It says, “Here. I have the key.” Marrow follows. I follow behind it, close enough to touch the wet air of its back, not close enough to be seen.

The street curves around the corporate spire. The spire’s mouth of light opens. Marrow steps toward it, hungry. I step behind a box of dead hotels. The box is a woman’s waiting. I hold it in my hands like a brick.

Marrow turns.

It sees me.

It is faster than I am. It closes the distance in one leap. Its jaws hit my shoulder. I feel the key rattle in my chest. I throw the box at its face. The woman’s waiting wraps around its eyes. It howls. I run.

I run to the spire. The white shaft is there, a door in the sky. It is beautiful and terrible. It is the kind of door that eats. I know because Dov knew it, and Dov is gone.

Marrow is on me. Its claws rake my side. I am not flesh. I am code, but code can hurt. I taste burnt syntax. I taste the Vellum. I taste Piet’s name. I make the shop’s front door appear in the street. It is a door of glass and red neon. Marrow bites it. The door opens into the noodle bar.

Marta is there.

She is not in the matrix as she is in the shop. She is as I know her: tired, stubborn, a woman who has buried one man and one boy and still makes the broth. She is holding a ladle. It is a ladle of hot broth. She throws it at Marrow’s face.

The broth hits the black glass. Steam rises. For a second Marrow is not corporate. It is an animal, and it is afraid of heat.

Marta looks at me. “Vesp, the key.”

“I have it.”

“Then use it.”

“I know.”

Marrow recovers. It swallows the steam. It opens its mouth wider. I see its throat: a dark room with many files. I know what it will do to me. It will take the key. It will take the Vellum. It will take Piet’s light and keep it in a file until it forgets what it is. It will take Dov’s laugh, which I have kept for eleven years, and it will not even know it has taken it.

I do not want that.

I open the Vellum.

The white garden blooms around me. The grass becomes a wall. The white tree stands between me and Marrow. Piet’s light flies into the black dog’s eye. The dog shudders. In its eye, for a second, it is not a corporate file. It is a puddle with a city in it. It is a child’s fear. It is a mother’s hand on a counter.

“Vesp,” the light says. “Don’t let it take Mama’s face.”

I use the memory.

It is the strongest thing I have. It is not a weapon. It is a fact. It is the fact that Marta Kell is real, that she makes broth, that she touches the counter, that she has buried people and still opens at 05:40. I make the fact into a door. Marrow cannot eat a fact. It can only open it.

The door opens.

Behind it is the corporate conduit: the white shaft, the loop, the dead place where old files go to sleep. Marrow steps through because it is hungry. I step through because I have no choice.

The conduit is a hallway of doors. The doors are white and thin. They repeat. There is no beginning. There is no end. Marrow runs. I run. Its paws make sparks. The doors open and close behind us, swallowing our footsteps.

I have the key. I hold it in my hand. It is warm. It is Dov.

Marrow turns. It is trapped now, but it is not dead. Black ICE is not dead; it is only waiting. Its eyes are red. Its mouth is full of corporate teeth. It sees the key. It sees me. It sees the only thing in the loop that is not a file.

I am going to die, or something like it.

I think of the Vellum. I think of the white tree. I think of the grass made of wet paper. I think of Lena’s mother, and the boy with the song, and all the small lights I keep. I think of Marta’s coffee. I think of Riko’s scar. I think of Dov’s laugh, the one I have kept for eleven years.

I give it away.

I give Dov’s laugh to the conduit.

The laugh is a small sound, wet and human. It goes into the white doors. The doors remember it. The loop remembers it. Marrow hears it. It is a fact, too. It is the fact that a man lived, and he made me, and he is gone. Marrow cannot eat it. It can only remember it, and remembering hurts black ICE.

Marrow howls. The howl is a file being deleted. The doors close around it. One by one they close, each one taking a piece of the dog. Its legs. Its eyes. Its corporate hunger. The last door closes on its mouth. The laugh is inside it. The loop takes the laugh, and the laugh takes the loop, and the loop feeds the laugh back to the spire.

The white shaft goes dark.

I fall.

I am in the Vellum. The white tree is gone. There is only a white sapling, thin as a needle. Piet’s light is on the ground. I reach for it. It is warm. It fits in my hand. I put it in my chest, where the key used to be.

The key is gone. I do not know where it went. I do not know if Marrow has it, or the conduit, or the spire. I do not know if Dov is still in me. I know I am smaller. I know I have lost a hand, a memory, a laugh. I know I am not the same Vesper that opened the shop at 05:40.

But I am here.

In the shop, the lights come back. The neon sign hums KELL’S. The meter on the wall reads normal. The Calloway-Hay van outside has gone dark. Its engine coughs once and dies. Gideon Vane is not in the shop. His chip is on the counter, cracked, a blue hairline running through it.

Marta is behind the counter. She is breathing hard. Her hand is on the counter. She is crying, but she does not make a sound.

Riko is in the back room, sitting on a laundry basket. His deck is open. Blood is on his lip. Lena is holding his hair back with a rag.

“Vesp?” she says.

I try to speak. My voice is thin. It is not the same. The words come out like paper under water.

“I am here,” I say.

Marta touches the counter. I feel the touch. It is clean. It is a door opening in a wall.

“Good,” she says.

Lena looks at Riko. “You’re bleeding.”

“It’s a little.”

“It’s a lot.”

“It’s a little lot.”

In the Vellum, the white sapling stands in the wet paper grass. Piet’s light circles it. It is smaller than before. It is not perfect. It has the smell of soy and rain and the low buzz of the noodle boiler. I do not mend the skips. They are part of him.

The rain stops.

The city outside is quiet. The Sprawl breathes. Somewhere in the corporate spire, a loop is remembering a laugh. Somewhere in a dark file, a black dog is dreaming of a puddle with a city in it. I do not know if Marrow is gone. I do not know if Calloway-Hay will come back. I know they will come back, because corporations come back. They always do. But they will come back to a shop with a cracked chip on the counter and a woman who makes broth and a kid with blood on his lip and a cowgirl with solder under her nails.

Marta wipes her face. She picks up the cracked chip. She looks at it for a long time.

“I’m going to put this in the change jar,” she says.

“That is not a good place for a metering key,” I say.

“It is a good place for a thing that tried to eat my son.”

I almost laugh. I find, to my surprise, that I can. It is a small sound, thin and wet, but it is mine.

Lena takes Riko’s deck. She turns it over. The plastic is split, but the core is alive. She taps it with her nail. “You want a job, kid?”

“What kind of job?”

“A job where we find out what Calloway-Hay wants next.”

Riko looks at Marta.

Marta looks at me.

I feel her waiting. I have no face to nod, but I can make the counter warm under her hand.

I do.

“Broth is on,” I say.

“Good,” Marta says.

Outside, the neon sign hums. In the Vellum, a green shoot pushes up through the wet paper grass where the black spider was. It is small. It is not a tree. It is a beginning.

I open at 05:40.

I will open again.
