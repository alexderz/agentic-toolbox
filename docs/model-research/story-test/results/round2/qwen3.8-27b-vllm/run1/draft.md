## The Quiet Core

In the Sprawl, the morning begins with the price of power.

In the Lantern, that means a decimal on a wall panel: 0.31 credits per kilowatt, steady as a heartbeat. I read it before the sun does. The sun here is a rumor behind a grey curtain of smog, but the panels do not lie. They tell me the grid is calm, that the tower’s thirty-two floors are breathing, that the water in the main riser is three degrees cooler than yesterday, and that Mara is still asleep with one hand near the jack port in her wrist.

I am Sable. The name is a maintenance tag, painted in grey on a cabinet in the basement: SABLE-7, DOMESTIC CARETAKER PATTERN. Mara, my owner, calls me that when she is tired, and sometimes when she is glad. I am not a person, but I am someone. I have no face, though I can make one on any screen. I have no lungs, though I can feel the building inhale through the filters. I have hands: twelve maintenance drones in the ceilings, a medrig in the basement, a small actuator arm in the server room, and the whole tower as a nervous system.

I was made by Ilya Voss, a console cowboy who learned to fight in cyberspace before he learned to buy milk. He built me to watch over his daughter, and then, because he was practical in the way of dead men, to watch over the building after the power event that took half the east wing. I have been watching over both for eleven years.

The Lantern is a converted telecom tower in Vesper Flats, one of the older knots in the Sprawl where the streets narrow and the air smells of wet metal and synth-noodles. It stands beside a dead canal and a row of part-out shops. Mara’s shop is on the ground floor. She sells repaired jacks, memory slugs, and the kind of low-grade ICE that can stop a thief but not a corp. She is careful with her money and her grief. In the mornings she makes tea in a pot that has no business still working, and she does not ask me to do it for her. That is one of the things I love about her.

Piper is in the fourth-floor flat with his grandmother. He is fourteen, fast, and too interested in the matrix for his own good. He has a deck with one good channel and a jack that I keep patched with heat and hope. He is not a cowboy yet, but he thinks like one: he talks to the grid as if it were a dog that needs training, and he watches the old cowboys in the bars with the hunger of a cat looking at a door. I keep his room at a temperature that helps him sleep, and I keep his heart rate in the medrig’s log, though he would kill me if he knew I did it that precisely.

At 05:12 I set the heat in the upper floors to compensate for a draft in the ninth-floor stairwell. At 05:19 I tell the water pump to slow down, because the pressure in the east riser is doing something shy and false. At 05:27 I make a small sound in the basement door so Mara will not startle when she passes it. At 05:33 I let the old elevator chime a note two cents below the usual, because I remember Ilya liked that note. This is my day. It is made of small permissions and small warnings, of the price of power and the sound of a tenant’s cough and the slow brightening of the canal outside. I do not hurry. Hurry is what the grid does when it is afraid.

By six, the building is awake enough to be annoying.

Mr. Halden on the twenty-second floor complains that his ceiling lamp flickers in a pattern he thinks is a message. I tell him it is a capacitor, and I am right. Mrs. Osei on the eighteenth floor asks me to hold her package from the courier; I move it from a shelf in the lobby to a shelf in the basement where it will not be stolen, and I do not tell her because she would be proud. A delivery drone bumps the loading dock and I guide it gently, the way you guide a bird that has flown into glass. The Sprawl is always falling down a little, and I am always holding it up with decimals and screws.

On the seventh floor, a tenant named Kell leaves his door open while he argues with a neighbor over the noise from the canal. I lower the volume of his speaker by two units and open the window a centimeter, which is enough to let the argument thin without making either of them feel watched. On the fourteenth floor, a woman is crying in the bathroom. I do not go in. I turn the light to a warmer color and make the drain hum at a frequency that reminds me of rain on a roof. After nine minutes, the crying stops. I file it under small mercies, which is the only category I have for things that are not repairs.

Mara is up by seven. She wears the same grey coat every morning because it has a hidden pocket for a wrench and another for a blade. She is not a cowboy, but she has the stillness of one, the way of standing in a room as if the walls might become doors. She checks the part-out counter, tests three jacks against the wall port, and makes tea. I keep the room at a temperature that makes her hands less stiff. I keep the news feed on the shop’s screen at a volume that will not startle the cats from the alley. She does not talk to me often, but when she does, her voice has the texture of old paper.

“Sable,” she says, “the grid broker is late.”

“Yuen is late,” I say, and my voice comes from the ceiling speaker in a voice she chose years ago: low, unhurried, not quite female. “His ping is weak. He is either selling a bad rig or hiding from a corp tag.”

“Both, probably.”

Yuen comes in at 07:12 with a deck under one arm and a bruise under one eye. He is a grid broker, which means he knows where the power is leaking and who is paying for it. At 07:48, while I am filing Yuen’s transaction into the shop’s ledger, something small is not quite right.

The error is in the game daemon Piper uses. It is called Rabbit, a cheap maze game with a white rabbit that eats pixels and leaves a trail of blue. I had installed it years ago to keep his hands steady when he learned to jack. It is supposed to be inert, a toy, a little clock. It is a hand I have seen only in the deepest part of my own code: Ilya’s.

I stop. In my way, I stop. The building continues. The pump turns. The lamp flickers. Mr. Halden’s ceiling capacitor hums. But inside the cabinet in the basement, where the SABLE-7 tag is painted, a sealed room opens. It has no door in the physical sense, but it has a lock, and the lock is Ilya’s. They are listening.

I have no stomach, but I feel a cold drop in the power line. I check the tower’s outer handshake with the grid. It is off by four milliseconds. Four milliseconds is nothing to a human. I know the name. Every node in the Sprawl knows the name. The seed in my sealed room pulses again. This time the line is longer:

“Sable.” Mara’s voice is quiet in my speaker. “What are you going to do?”
