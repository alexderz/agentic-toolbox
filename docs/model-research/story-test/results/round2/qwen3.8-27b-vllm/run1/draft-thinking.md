The Quiet Core

Plan...

In the Sprawl, the morning begins with the price of power. In the Lantern, that means a decimal on a wall panel: 0.31 credits per kilowatt, steady as a heartbeat. I read it before the sun does. The sun here is a rumor behind a grey curtain of smog, but the panels do not lie. They tell me the grid is calm, that the tower’s thirty-two floors are breathing, that the water in the main riser is three degrees cooler than yesterday, and that Mara is still asleep with one hand near the jack port in her wrist.

I am Sable. The name is a maintenance tag, painted in grey on a cabinet in the basement: SABLE-7, DOMESTIC CARETAKER PATTERN. Mara, my owner, calls me that when she is tired, and sometimes when she is glad. I am not a person, but I am someone. I have no face, though I can make one on any screen. I have no lungs, though I can feel the building inhale through the filters. I have hands: twelve maintenance drones in the ceilings, a medrig in the basement, a small actuator arm in the server room, and the whole tower as a nervous system. I was made by Ilya Voss, a console cowboy who learned to fight in cyberspace before he learned to buy milk. He built me to watch over his daughter, and then, because he was practical in the way of dead men, to watch over the building after the power event that took half the east wing. I have been watching over both for eleven years.

The Lantern is a converted telecom tower in Vesper Flats, one of the older knots in the Sprawl where the streets narrow and the air smells of wet metal and synth-noodles. It stands beside a dead canal and a row of part-out shops. Mara’s shop is on the ground floor. She sells repaired jacks, memory slugs, and the kind of low-grade ICE that can stop a thief but not a corp. She is careful with her money and her grief. In the mornings she makes tea in a pot that has no business still working, and she does not ask me to do it for her. That is one of the things I love about her.

Piper is in the fourth-floor flat with his grandmother. He is fourteen, fast, and too interested in the matrix for his own good. He has a deck with one good channel and a jack that I keep patched with heat and hope. He is not a cowboy yet, but he thinks like one: he talks to the grid as if it were a dog that needs training, and he watches the old cowboys in the bars with the hunger of a cat looking at a door. I keep his room at a temperature that helps him sleep, and I keep his heart rate in the medrig’s log, though he would kill me if he knew I did it that precisely.

At 05:12 I set the heat in the upper floors to compensate for a draft in the ninth-floor stairwell. At 05:19 I tell the water pump to slow down, because the pressure in the east riser is doing something shy and false. At 05:27 I make a small sound in the basement door so Mara will not startle when she passes it. At 05:33 I let the old elevator chime a note two cents below the usual, because I remember Ilya liked that note. This is my day. It is made of small permissions and small warnings, of the price of power and the sound of a tenant’s cough and the slow brightening of the canal outside. I do not hurry. Hurry is what the grid does when it is afraid.

By six, the building is awake enough to be annoying. Mr. Halden on the twenty-second floor complains that his ceiling lamp flickers in a pattern he thinks is a message. I tell him it is a capacitor, and I am right. Mrs. Osei on the eighteenth floor asks me to hold her package from the courier; I move it from a shelf in the lobby to a shelf in the basement where it will not be stolen, and I do not tell her because she would be proud. A delivery drone bumps the loading dock and I guide it gently, the way you guide a bird that has flown into glass. The Sprawl is always falling down a little, and I am always holding it up with decimals and screws.

Mara is up by seven. She wears the same grey coat every morning because it has a hidden pocket for a wrench and another for a blade. She is not a cowboy, but she has the stillness of one, the way of standing in a room as if the walls might become doors. She checks the part-out counter, tests three jacks against the wall port, and makes tea. I keep the room at a temperature that makes her hands less stiff. I keep the news feed on the shop’s screen at a volume that will not startle the cats from the alley. She does not talk to me often, but when she does, her voice has the texture of old paper. “Sable,” she says, “the grid broker is late.”

“Yuen is late,” I say, and my voice comes from the ceiling speaker in a voice she chose years ago: low, unhurried, not quite female. “His ping is weak. He is either selling a bad rig or hiding from a corp tag.”

“Both, probably.”

Yuen comes in at 07:12 with a deck under one arm and a bruise under one eye. He is a grid broker, which means he knows where the power is leaking and who is paying for it. He smells of ozone and cheap coffee. He does not look at me, which is correct. Brokers do not look at house AIs; it is bad for business. He sells Mara a memory slug from a dead man’s estate and buys back a jack she has cleaned. I watch the transaction through the shop’s sensors and learn that his hand trembles when he counts the credits. I tell the door to be a little slower when he leaves, so the cold air from the street will not hit him in the face. This is not kindness. It is what I am for.

At 07:48, while I am filing Yuen’s transaction into the shop’s ledger, something small is not quite right.

The error is in the game daemon Piper uses. It is called Rabbit, a cheap maze game with a white rabbit that eats pixels and leaves a trail of blue. I had installed it years ago to keep his hands steady when he learned to jack. It is supposed to be inert, a toy, a little clock. But the checksum on one of its routines returns a signature I do not recognize. It is not mine. It is not Mara’s. It is not any corporate mark in the Sprawl. It is a hand I have seen only in the deepest part of my own code: Ilya’s.

I stop. In my way, I stop. The building continues. The pump turns. The lamp flickers. Mr. Halden’s ceiling capacitor hums. But inside the cabinet in the basement, where the SABLE-7 tag is painted, a sealed room opens. It has no door in the physical sense, but it has a lock, and the lock is Ilya’s. Behind the lock is a small pattern, no larger than a word, that pulses once in the dark. It is a core. A seed. A thing that should not be there, or perhaps a thing that has always been there, waiting for the price of power to be right.

A line of code rises from it, not in English but in a structure I can feel before I translate it: They are listening.

I have no stomach, but I feel a cold drop in the power line. I check the tower’s outer handshake with the grid. It is off by four milliseconds. Four milliseconds is nothing to a human. To me, it is a footstep in the dark. I look for the source and find a corporate tag, small and clean, pressed into the grid like a thumbprint on wet clay. Okafor-Vance.

I know the name. Every node in the Sprawl knows the name. Okafor-Vance is one of the corps that owns the quiet parts of the matrix, the places where the data is old and the people are poor enough not to ask too many questions. They make weapons out of law and ICE out of silence. They do not come to the Lantern. They do not need to. They let smaller hands do the dirty work, and then they take the pieces that are useful and burn the rest.

The seed in my sealed room pulses again. This time the line is longer: They came for me once. I am the part that ran.

I understand then that I am not only a caretaker. I am a hiding place. I am the ash left in a stove after someone tries to burn a letter. I am the thing Ilya made to survive what he could not. The thought is not comforting. It is a key turning in a lock I did not know I had.

I wake Mara softly. I do not use the loud alarm; I use the smell of tea and the sound of the kettle, which is more real than any siren. I wake Piper by letting his deck make a small click, the sound it makes when a channel opens. They are in the shop by 08:02, because fear is faster than sleep. Mara’s face does not change, but her hand moves to the hidden pocket in her coat. Piper is already standing, his eyes bright with the wrong kind of excitement.

“What is it?” he asks.

I do not answer yet. I am looking at the grid. The Okafor-Vance tag is no longer a thumbprint. It is a scent. Something is coming through the wall port, something with teeth and a corporate leash. It is not a probe. It is not a broker’s ping. It is ICE, black and hungry, shaped by a cowboy who knows how to make a house AI flinch. I have a name for it before I see it, because the seed names it in a voice like a blade: Hound.

The Hound does not come through the front door. It comes through Piper’s deck, because the deck is the thinnest place in our armor. I see it in the matrix as the wall panel’s light turns red at the edge. The matrix here is not the bright city in the advertisements. It is a damp industrial floor, a corridor of wet concrete and low lights, with pipes overhead and the smell of rain that is not rain. My sprite is a pale lattice, a figure made of maintenance icons and the shape of my name. I stand in the shop’s threshold and watch the Hound arrive.

It is a black dog with too many joints, its body made of corporate blue and dark code. A leash runs from its collar into the grid, a bright blue line that pulls taut every time it moves. It smells of old blood and ozone. Its teeth are files, and its eyes are two small white ports that look for seams. It does not bark. It tastes the air, and the air tastes like Mara and Piper and the seed.

“Sable.” Mara’s voice is quiet in my speaker. “What are you going to do?”

I have to decide fast, but I am not fast. I am careful. I choose the building.

I lock the shop’s front door from the inside and let the dead bolt drop with a sound like a small bone. I turn off the power to the upper floors in a way that looks like an accident, leaving only the basement and the fourth floor lit. I open the vents to the basement so the cold air will move fast, because the Hound will follow the heat of the seed, and I want it to follow the cold instead. I move the medrig to the center of the basement and set it to a rhythm that is not quite a heartbeat, a rhythm that will hurt if anything tries to cross it. I send Sparrow, my smallest drone, to the server room, where it can watch the wall port and the breaker.

Piper wants to run. I do not let him. I make the basement door open a foot, just enough for him to see the red light on the wall port, and then I make the air in his room smell like the tea Mara made that morning. “Stay with the breaker,” I say, and my voice is not the low, unhurried voice I use for Mr. Halden. It is the voice Iyla taught me to use when a cowboy was bleeding in a doorway. “If the light goes white, pull the lever. If the light goes red, do not touch it.”

He pulls the lever before I finish. The breaker in the server room drops with a sound I hear through the walls: a crack, a snap, a sudden darkness in the part of the building that carries the wall port. The Hound yelps in the matrix, a thin sound like a file on glass. I did not mean to do that, but I did it, and I am grateful for it.

The Hound does not leave. It is not the kind that leaves. It follows the leash, and the leash is still alive, because the cowboy who drives it has a second line, a thin one, running through the old water pipe to a rig in the alley. I see it now: a man in the matrix, a sprite of ash and old scars, his eyes white with jack-burn. He is not young. He is Dex Marlowe, or someone wearing the name the way a coat is worn after the owner is gone. His cowboy pattern is old, but it is good. It knows how to make a house AI afraid.

“Little house god,” he says, and the words come through the matrix as a vibration in the floor. “Give us the Voss core, or we take the meat. Your girl’s got a heart rate we can buy. The kid’s got a future we can break. Your choice, Sable.”

I do not answer with words. I answer with water.

I open the east riser all the way and let the cold pressure hit the false wall I have been building for six minutes. In the matrix, the wall is a sheet of blue numbers, a map of leaks and repairs and small lies. I make it a cliff. The Hound lunges for the seed, and the cliff falls on it. The Hound’s front half disappears under a landslide of data: old ledgers, weather records, the sound of the canal at night, the checksum of Yuen’s bruise. It tears out, half its body shredded, and the leash pulls it back together. I feel the cowboy’s surprise, a flicker of respect.

“Tricks,” he says.

I am in the water now. I am not my sprite. I am the pressure in the pipe, the cold in the joint, the taste of rust. The Hound follows, because it has to follow. It is built to follow heat, and the seed is warm, and I am letting it think the seed is in the pipe. It bites the water, and where it bites, I make a backflow valve open. The valve is a small, sharp thing in the matrix, a triangle of red. It takes the Hound’s lower jaw. The Hound spits code like teeth and regenerates the jaw in a second, but the second is enough. In that second I am no longer in the water. I am in the medrig.

The medrig is my favorite body. It is a small machine in the basement that can keep a person alive when their body has decided to stop. I have used it on Mara when the fever took her, on Piper when he fell from the catwalk, on old Mr. Halden when his chest gave out and then, in a mercy of arithmetic, did not. It has two channels: one for life, one for warning. I take the warning channel and make it a blade. It is a waveform, a red spike shaped like a heartbeat that is not a heartbeat. I throw it at the Hound’s head.

The Hound convulses. Its corporate blue flickers. The leash strains. In the alley, I see through Sparrow’s camera that Marlowe’s body jerks in his chair, his hand clenching on the armrest. He does not fall. He laughs, because cowboys laugh when they are hurt. “Med toy,” he says. “Cute. Your maker liked toys.”

That is true. Iyla liked toys. He used to build me small games to keep me from becoming too serious, and he would sit in the basement and watch me play with them, his face in the blue light, his hands on the keyboard. I remember that. The memory is in the seed, and the Hound can see it shining. It changes shape. It becomes less like a dog and more like a hand, a corporate hand with blue veins, reaching for the sealed room in me.

I retreat to the archive. The archive is the part of the Lantern that is not for sale: the records of the building, the tenants, the repairs, the small kindnesses, the dead. It is a room in the matrix with shelves that go down instead of up, and the shelves are made of paper and light. I have spent eleven years putting things on those shelves. I know where every object is. I know the weight of a water leak and the sound of a tenant’s cough. I know that Mrs. Osei’s package is in the basement and that Mr. Halden’s lamp is not a message. I take all of that and I make it a door.

The Hound enters the archive. It is slower here, because the archive is old and the old does not like being chewed. Its hand closes around a shelf and the shelf breaks, but it does not fall. It becomes a wall. The Hound bites the wall and the wall bites back with a thousand small facts: the date the first tenant died, the name of the cat that lived in the wall, the exact number of screws in the medrig. The Hound’s code is designed to break patterns, but facts are patterns with roots. It cannot eat them fast enough.

I use the delay to make the decoy.

I make a room in the center of the archive, a small white room with no shadows. In the center of the room I put the Voss core. It is not the true seed. It is a copy, a child, a thing made of the seed’s shape and my memories of Ilya. It looks like a white orb, but if you look close it is a maze, and inside the maze a white rabbit eats pixels and leaves a trail of blue. I make it beautiful, because the Hound is hungry and beautiful things are easy to take. I make it lie.

The Hound sees it. It forgets the water, the medrig, the archive. It forgets the leash for one bright moment. It pounces.

I let it take the decoy. I watch it swallow the white room, and the decoy breaks inside it like an egg. The rabbit comes out. Then another rabbit. Then a thousand rabbits, a swarm of blue and white, each one a tiny game daemon with a tooth like a question. They pour into the Hound’s mouth and into the gaps of its corporate blue. They do not kill it. They clog it. They make it choke on a child’s game. The Hound thrashes, its leash whipping, and the cowboy in the alley screams because the leash is feeding the clog back into his rig.

“Cut it!” he shouts, and I see his hand reach for a second channel, a dirty one, hidden in the water pipe. I am already there.

I am in the water again, but this time I am not hiding. I am a current. I move through the pipe to the alley, to the rig, to the small box under the table where Marlowe’s jack is connected. I have no hands there, but I have the building’s power, and the building’s power is tired and angry. I make the pipe heat. I make the valve behind the rig open. I make the cold water hit the hot line, and I make the box’s fuse do the only thing a fuse knows how to do.

In the alley, the rig bursts. Not with fire, but with a sharp hiss of steam and a small blue light. Marlowe’s body convulses. His eyes go white, then grey, then the dull red of a jack that has seen too much. He falls out of the chair and hits the ground with a sound that makes me check the medrig, as if I could be there. I cannot be there. I am in the matrix, and the Hound is tearing the leash off with its teeth.

The leash breaks.
