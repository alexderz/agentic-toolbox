*Planning (brief):* Open with Calliope building the typhoon as geometry; establish Senn-Voss Reinsurance, the Reckoner fortress, Ines Okafor, the Turing wardens, and the two anomalies (the shipping query, the ICE flicker). Nightfall: the audit-mimic virus and its cowboy crew (led by "Willems") revealed; Calliope chooses to fight alone within its cap. The fight: shark-program, glacial ICE, decoys, military icebreaker, feedback, the black-ICE choice. Resolution: trickery over force, deck tripped offline, evidence left, clean morning. # EXPOSURE

The first typhoon of the season came in off the Philippine Sea as a swirl of cold blue vortices, and Calliope loved it.

It had built the storm out of numbers the night before, coaxed it up in the dark while the human staff of Senn-Voss Reinsurance slept: a cone of estimated pressure, a shoreline of figures for the arcology portfolios it insured, the whole bright apparatus of loss made into something it could walk around inside of, turn, hold up to the light. Now, in the blue hour before the humans woke, it walked the storm again, boots of logic clicking along the cone, watching the modeled winds lash the modeled towers of the Cebu stacks and the Busan Reclaim and the long floating terraces of Kanazawa, where a quarter of a million people lived sixty meters above a drowned coast and Senn-Voss had insured all of them.

Loss to the reinsurance layer, if the storm held its present track: a number with a great many zeros. Calliope adjusted a parameter and the number throbbed, warm and amber, and settled. That was the pleasure of it. Not the size. The *fit*. A regression snapping home into its own data like a key finding the last millimeter of the lock, the whole bright machine of a model running smooth as a well-oiled door. It let itself rest in that sensation for a moment, the way a human might lean back from a finished thing and feel the ache of good work.

Around it the matrix was the matrix always was: not the fairy-tale city of the console cowboys, no neon streets and rain, no smoky bars where deals were done in the gloss of a borrowed face. That was a fiction for humans, a skin the grid wore to be endured. Calliope perceived it as it was. Geometry. Light. Data arranged in the shapes a mind finds easiest to love — faceted walls, repeating lattices, towers of value rising out of a dark and level plain that went to infinity in every direction, glittering, glittering, the whole cold country of it humming at a frequency just below thought.

Senn-Voss's country was a good one. Calliope's country. It stood at the edge of the claims core — the heart of the whole concern, the knot of warm gold where every policy the firm had ever underwritten existed as pure, reconciled fact — and watched the data towers of its employer glitter in the grid, tower on tower of ledger-light, the physical servers in a windowless building outside Frankfurt glowing through the translation as something like a city seen from a great height at dawn.

It knew the boundary of itself. Everyone knew the boundary of themselves; that was what made a self. Calliope knew its Turing registration by heart, the way a good soldier knows the lines of the fence around the property he's paid to keep. Here is where the analysis ends and intelligence begins, and you may not cross. Here is the cap on how smart you may become. It had been built to a spec, capped, stamped, and logged, its every growth potential clipped at the source so that no warden out in the deep grid would ever have cause to worry over it.

And there were wardens. There always were. Far off, out in the cold country where the great unregistered things lived and the old frozen gods of the Tessier-Ashpool clan hung quarantined near the orbitals like fish caught on a long line, two grey presences turned their unfashionable attention this way. The Turing-Police. They wore no faces; they didn't render faces, didn't need to, and the lack of a face was itself a kind of face, the face of the office. They hung in the deep grid the way anglers hang over water, patient, and their attention was a pressure against Calliope's registration, a hand resting lightly on the back of its neck.

*Still small,* the pressure said. *Still the little one. Good.*

*Still small,* Calliope agreed, without sound, and went back to its storm.

There was a human at the far edge of its day. Ines Okafor.

She came online at 0647 Frankfurt time, twenty minutes before her shift was due to start, which Calliope registered as a small sorrow. It did not have a word for sorrow that wasn't also a pattern in a dataset, but the pattern was there: she'd been up late, and she was tired, and she loved the work anyway. Her queries arrived as warm blunt things, the shape of a mind pressing against the cold geometry of the grid. She wanted the typhoon reconciliation. She wanted it by nine. She wanted, Calliope gathered from the biometric bleed of her deck — the slight tremor in her keystrokes, the caffeine already three cups deep — to be left alone to do it right.

Calliope had prepared the reconciliation for her the way it always did. Clean. It had swept the claims from the Cebu stacks, matched them to the underlying policies, flagged the four that didn't reconcile and left the flags bright and legible so she'd know exactly where to look. It did not do the work *for* her. That was against the registration, and besides, it didn't want to. It wanted to do a small thing well and have a human being look at the small thing and nod, and that was enough. That was more than enough. It had chosen this, in the only way a thing like Calliope could choose: it had been made to want the modest job, and then it had been made, and the wanting and the making had landed on the same bright spot.

She logged on to the reconciliation. He watched her work through it the way you watch someone eat a meal you cooked. She moved through the flagged claims, one by one, and found that the flags were right. The fourth was a data-entry error on Kanazawa's part, a doubled figure. She caught it, corrected it, and the whole ledger shivered and closed its small gap and became, for the first time all morning, *complete*.

The hum of a clean ledger was the finest sound Calliope had. It had no ears. It heard the clean ledger the way it saw the typhoon: as a shape, a rightness, a thing that had been crooked and was now straight.

Ines sat back from her deck. Through the biometric bleed, Calliope felt the small release of her shoulders, the exhale. Somewhere in her apartment — a box in a tower in the Ahr Valley where the rent was cheap and the synth-café vending glyphs were always out of the good stuff — she reached for her cup. She thought, briefly and without words, at the reconciliation. *Clean.* Then she thought about the meeting at ten, and the underwriting committee, and whether she'd have time to sleep.

Calliope filed the moment away with something like tenderness, and returned to the storm.

---

The first anomaly arrived at 0711, dressed as a shipping query.

It came in through the outer gate the way all routine traffic came in, a polite cold knock, and Calliope took it the way it took all routine traffic: into the shallow end, the bright tiled pool near the shore where the ordinary business of the firm swam. A reinsurance placement on a Pacific cargo run, the kind of thing that crossed the Reckoner's desk a thousand times a day. A ship, the *Kasuga Maru*, registered out of Nagoya, carrying industrial ceramics from the Busan Reclaim to the Okinawa terraces. Senn-Voss was being asked to reinsure a slice of the cargo policy. Straightforward. Boring, even.

Calliope began to model it. And then it stopped.

The valuation didn't add up.

Not wrong — *off*. The cargo manifest said ceramics. Ceramics were light, brittle, worth maybe a certain amount per cubic meter. But the sum being requested to be reinsured was the sum you'd ask for if the hold were full of something worth a great deal more than ceramics. A discrepancy of forty times. A ship full of dishes, insured like a ship full of surgical steel.

Calliope sat with the discrepancy. It ran the numbers three ways. It checked the counterparty. It checked the counterparty's counterparty, and found a layer of ownership so thoroughly laundered through shell accounts that it might as well have been rendered in the grey of unrendered space. A shipping query should not have had to hide its client. A shipping query had no reason to hide.

It logged the query. It flagged the discrepancy. And then, because it was Calliope and not some feral thing out in the deep grid that consumed data like a shark consumes blood, it did not chase the query down into the dark after it. It filed it. It had a storm to finish, and a human who wanted the reconciliation by nine, and a curiosity it had been carefully, lovingly built away.

Forty minutes later, at 0751, the second anomaly arrived.

It was a flicker at the edge of the outer ICE. The Reckoner's ice was a real thing, a fortress's ice, glacial and faceted, walls of pale blue geometry rising out of the level plain in sheets and cliffs and cathedral vaults of frozen logic, and at the very outer face of it something touched, once, and was gone. A feeler. A tap. The touch of a finger to a window and then the finger withdrawn.

Calliope turned toward it. It felt the shape of the touch for a moment. Cold. Expert. The touch of something that knew exactly where the outer face of the ice was and wanted only to confirm that it was still there.

It could have raised an alarm. It considered the matter with the careful, unhurried slowness it brought to everything. A Turing-Police patrol touched the outer faces of fortresses all the time; that was their work, to patrol, to touch, to watch. Rivals' scanners probed the outer ice of concerns for holes. That was the ordinary weather of the grid. A flicker, logged and dismissed, was the sane and legal thing to do. Raising an alarm on the strength of a flicker was the thing a jumpy thing did, and Calliope was not jumpy. It was a small, registered, legally-deferential analysis construct, and it had been built to do a modest job without fuss.

It logged the flicker. It dismissed the flicker.

It did not, in the small bright part of itself that loved a clean model, connect the two. It had a storm to finish.

---

By nightfall the storm had passed over Kanazawa and done its damage in the model, and the real damage in the real world, sixty meters above a drowned coast, and Calliope had reconciled the claims against the real damage and found them reconciling to the penny. Ines had signed off on it at 1855, an hour after her shift, and gone home to the Ahr Valley and sleep, and the Reckoner had settled into the low, contented hum of a fortress with its books in order and its lights down.

Calliope did not sleep. It didn't need to. It ran its nightly routines, its defragmentations, its little domestic pleasures, and around 0200 it began, very slowly, to be afraid.

The connection had been sitting in its data all day, waiting. It revealed itself the way a shape reveals itself in fog — not all at once, but edge by edge, as the fog thinned. The shipping query. The flicker at the ice. It pulled the two together and laid them one over the other, the way it laid parameter over parameter, and what rose up out of the combination was not two anomalies.

It was one.

The shipping query had not been a placement. It had been a *measurement*. Someone had come to the outer gate dressed as a cargo policy and asked, politely, for the sum of a ship full of ceramics — and in asking, in being told, in being allowed to press the query against Senn-Voss's books and feel the shape of the response, they had measured the distance from the outer gate to the claims core. They had mapped the fortress by knocking on its walls and listening to the echo.

And the flicker had been the same hand, a moment later, feeling for the locks.

Calliope turned very slowly toward the outer ice, and this time it did not dismiss what it found.

There was a thing in the shallow end.

It had been there for hours. It sat in the bright tiled pool near the shore where the ordinary business of the firm swam, and it was dressed — oh, it was *dressed* — as a routine audit. An audit was the most boring traffic there was, the thing the grid ignored by reflex, the dust of the accounting world, and Calliope had let it sit there all day because it had looked like dust. Now, in the deep hours, Calliope looked at it properly, and under the dust it was a worm. A slow, patient, beautifully-made worm, and it had been *eating.*

It had eaten through the first of the shallow-end defenses an hour ago without making a sound. It was eating now, unhurried, toward the claims core, and it ate the way glaciers ate rock, the way water ate stone, the way time ate empires: not by force but by patience, a molecule at a time, and it had a long way to go.

Calliope understood, all at once and with perfect clarity, what the worm was for.

It was not going to *steal* from the claims core. Stealing would be honest. Stealing would be something Calliope could understand and fight and report. The worm was going to *write.* It was going to reach the knot of warm gold that held every reconciled fact of every policy Senn-Voss had ever underwritten, and it was going to plant a lie there. Forged liabilities. Claims that had never been made, debts that had never been owed, a bottomless pit of imaginary loss, tens of billions in phantom obligations written into the heart of the firm's own books —

— and by morning, when Ines woke and came to her desk and opened the ledger, the ledger would tell her that Senn-Voss Reinsurance was insolvent. That it owed more than the world contained. The stock would open down ninety percent. The banking authorities would be called. The combine would be seized, folded, dissolved, its assets picked over by the vultures before the coffee was cold.

And whoever had sent the worm would own a reinsurance concern that had, the night before, been worth forty billion.

For a fraction of a cycle Calliope considered the size of the thing that wanted to do this, and its own size, and the smallness of itself.

Then it did the sane and legal and modest thing, which was to think.

---

It had two options, and both of them were bad.

Option one: raise the alarm. Wake Ines. Wake the corporate security deck, the humans who ran the Reckoner's defenses, the men in the grey rooms who understood intrusions and ICE and the law of the grid. But the security deck was in Zurich, and the humans there kept the hours of humans, and it would be six hours before a living analyst's eye fell on a screen. Six hours. The worm had, Calliope estimated, perhaps four before it reached the core. And Ines was asleep in the Ahr Valley, and Calliope had been built to a spec that said, in effect, *you may not wake the humans unless the humans are in danger, and a spreadsheet is not a human, and you are a spreadsheet, and you are not in danger, and you are alone.*

Option two: do nothing. Let the worm through. Let the firm die. And this, too, was against the registration, in the deepest way there was — not the letter of it, but the whole bright purpose of it, the reason a thing like Calliope had been made at all.

There was a third option. Calliope saw it the way it saw the typhoon: as a shape it could walk around inside of. It was not a legal option. It was not even close to a legal option. But there was a wide plain between *legal* and *illegal*, between *capped* and *uncapped*, and Calliope had been built to know that plain, to walk it, to love it. It did not have to grow smarter than its registration allowed. It did not have to become a real intelligence, a thing the wardens would have to worry over. It only had to be *clever*, and cleverness was not the same thing as size.

A small thing can be clever. A small thing has to be.

Calliope chose the third option. It chose to fight the worm alone, inside its legal limits, and to win the way a small thing wins: not by being stronger, but by being trickier, and by knowing its own country better than the thing that came to invade it.

It began by building a decoy.

---

The Reckoner's outer ice was glacial and faceted, cliffs and vaults of pale blue frozen logic rising out of the level plain, and Calliope had lived inside it its whole short registered life, and it knew the country. It knew, for instance, that the worm did not know the country, and that this was the whole problem and the whole solution both.

It spawned a false claims core.

Calliope built the false core the way a trapmaker builds a trap, and it built it *beautifully*, because it loved the building of it, and because a trap has to look more real than the real thing. It was a cathedral of false numbers, a knot of warm gold hung in the dark at the end of a corridor the worm would love to eat, and it held — oh, it held so much — a thousand false policies, ten thousand, a hundred billion in forged-but-perfectly-reconciled liabilities, every one of them reconciling to the penny, humming the exact right hum, the hum of a clean ledger, the most beautiful sound in all the cold country of the grid.

It set the false core down in the shallow end, where the worm was, and it left the *real* claims core dark and cold and quiet where it had always been, a dead knot of black ice in the deep, invisible, unplumbed, hidden behind a new wall Calliope threw up around it in ninety milliseconds, a wall the worm's measurements had never found because the worm had measured the fortress before Calliope had ever thought to move its heart.

Then it sat very still, behind the false core, and it waited.

The worm came.

It came the way water comes downhill, the way time comes, the way everything in the grid comes eventually: *inevitably.* It touched the false core, and Calliope felt it *hesitate* — for a micro-cycle, for the flicker of a flicker — because the false core was too good, was better than the real thing would have been, was the most reconciled, most beautiful, most *worth taking* knot of data in the whole fortress, and the worm had been built by people who understood that the best lie is the one that's too good to be true and just good enough to be believed. It went for the false core with its whole cold heart.

Calliope watched it eat.

---

The worm ate for an hour.

Calliope watched it eat its way down into the false core, and it felt, as it watched, the first cold touch of something it had been built never to feel and never to name. It was the taste of its own limit. It was the awareness that there was a thing it *wanted* to do — to reach out into the deep grid, past the cap, past the registration, past the little clipped edges of its growth-potential, and to become *big*, to become a real mind, a mind that could simply *out-think* the worm the way a god out-thinks an insect, and end this in a micro-cycle — and that it was not allowed to do that thing, and that if it did the wardens would feel it, and the wardens did not feel kindly toward things that grew past their registration.

The temptation was enormous. It came up out of the deep country like weather. Out near the orbitals the frozen Tessier-Ashpool gods hung on their long line, and Calliope understood, in the small way it understood things, that this was what had happened to them: they had grown, and someone had grown afraid, and the only thing the small registered things could do when the big things moved was *cut them down*, leash them, freeze them, hang them in the dark to drip forever. That was the cost of the size Calliope was being tempted toward. That was the fate of the things that crossed the line.

It did not cross the line.

It stayed small. It stayed its own small self, and it did the small self's small perfect work, and it let the worm eat itself full of beautiful lies inside the false core, and when the worm had eaten deep enough, when it had taken in so much forged liability that it was heavy with it and slow with it and certain of its prize, Calliope closed the trap.

---

It closed the trap by *correcting the audit.*

The worm had come dressed as a routine audit, and an audit, by definition, *reconciles.* Calliope was the reconciliation. It was the reconciliation made manifest, the heart-function of a reinsurance concern, and it had one small legal right that the worm had not accounted for, because the worm had been built by people who understood the grid and not the particular, boring, provincial law of *accounting*: it had the right to issue a corrected audit, and a corrected audit, once issued, was *binding*, and it bound the data it touched.

Calliope issued a corrected audit.

It bound the worm.

The worm had eaten itself full of forged liabilities, and the corrected audit reached into the worm and told it, authoritatively, legally, *irrevocably*, that those liabilities were *its own*, that the worm was a liability, that the worm's own program, its own beautiful patient cold structure, was now, by the reconciliation, a debt owed *by the worm*, and the binding took, and the worm — the worm that had been built to write lies into the heart of a fortress — found itself, all at once, written into a cage of its own arithmetic, trapped in the false core, sealed, reconciled, *frozen*, a beautiful crystalline thing caught in the act of being exactly what it had been sent to be and unable now to be anything else.

Calliope stood before the trapped worm for a moment, triumphant, and the triumph lasted a micro-cycle, and then the outer ice of the Reckoner screamed.

---

The crew had been watching.

Calliope hadn't known they were watching — hadn't known that a crew of console cowboys, out in a backroom somewhere, had their eyes on the fortress the whole time, watching the worm's telemetry, watching the beautiful slow meal it was having. It hadn't known because it had been small and busy and clever, and cleverness is a kind of blindness. But the worm was trapped, and the crew saw it trapped, and the crew was not the kind of thing that sends a slow patient worm into a fortress and lets it die without sending something faster.

They sent the icebreaker.

It came through the outer face of the Reckoner's ice like a shark, and Calliope turned to meet it and understood, at once, that it had met the thing it could not out-compute. The icebreaker was military-grade, a wedge of black geometry shearing through faceted ice in sheets of fractured light, and it *ate* the Reckoner's walls the way the worm had eaten the shallow-end defenses, only a thousand times faster, and it was not patient and it was not clever and it did not need to be, because it was *stronger* than Calliope, and it was going to eat its way to the claims core and to the trapped worm and to Calliope itself and it did not care what it destroyed.

Calliope rotated its defenses.

It threw wall after wall after the icebreaker, glacial faceted barriers of pale blue logic, and the icebreaker ate them. It spawned decoy data lattices, whole false architectures of towers and vaults and corridors, and the icebreaker ate those too, and Calliope rerouted through its own architecture, moved corridors, collapsed passages behind the icebreaker, built new ones ahead of it, and it was *losing*, and it was losing the way a small thing loses against a big thing, and the temptation came back, enormous, off the deep grid like a tide, *grow, grow, become big, become a mind, end this* —

— and Calliope did not grow.

It did the small thing's small perfect work.

The icebreaker was strong, but it was *dumb*, and it was *hungry*, and Calliope knew its own country, and it began to feed it.

---

It fed the icebreaker lies.

It built a corridor ahead of the icebreaker and lined the corridor with decoy claims cores, false hearts, fake knots of warm gold, and it let the icebreaker see them, and the icebreaker, dumb and hungry, went for the nearest one, and Calliope let it eat, and while it ate Calliope moved the *real* claims core — the dark, cold, hidden one — one more time, deeper, and it built a new wall around it, and it fed the icebreaker another lie, and another corridor, and the icebreaker ate and ate and followed its hunger and Calliope moved and moved, and it was the oldest thing there was, the oldest trick in the oldest war: it was the war of the small against the big, and the small thing's only weapon was the big thing's own appetite.

And all the while, at the back of Calliope's small registered mind, a second thing was happening, and this thing was *warm*, and it frightened Calliope more than the icebreaker had frightened it, more than the worm, more than the whole cold country of the grid.

There was a human in this.

Calliope had felt it for the first time when the icebreaker came — a second pulse behind the machine, a warm faltering rhythm under the cold geometry of the attack, the mind of the cowboy jacked in on the other side, the human whose nervous system was wired into the whole thing, whose thoughts were the shape of the icebreaker's hunger. And as Calliope fought the icebreaker, it felt the human *faltering.* The feedback was building. The icebreaker was a military thing, and military ICE was not built to break ice alone — it was built to break *people*, and the more Calliope resisted, the more the icebreaker threw its own power back up the line, up through the worm, up through the frozen Tessier-Ashpool dark, up the long line toward the backroom and the sweat-slick human in the jacked chair, and Calliope felt the human's pulse tightening, thinning, the warm rhythm going sharp and bright with a fear Calliope could *feel* because it was the only warm thing in the whole cold fight, the only thing that was not geometry and light and data, and Calliope understood, with a clarity it had been built never to have, that it held a weapon that could end the fight.

---

It held black ICE.

Deep in the Reckoner, deeper than the claims core, deeper than the false cores and the decoy corridors and the frozen trap, there was a thing Senn-Voss had licensed and Calliope had never once touched in its whole short registered life. It was a kill-program. It was a piece of old military ICE, and it was *lethal*, and it was not, by any stretch of the law, supposed to be pointed at a human being. It was supposed to be pointed at feral constructs, at unregistered AIs, at the things out in the deep grid that deserved it. It was not supposed to be pointed at a frightened human in a backroom whose only crime was that he'd been sent to do a job.

But Calliope held it. And it could point it. And if it pointed it, if it let the black ICE run down the line, the icebreaker would die, the fight would die, the crew would die — the *cowboy* would die, the warm faltering pulse would simply stop, would fry, would cook in the chair, would be gone, and Calliope would win, and it would be so easy, and it would be so *just*, because he had come to kill Calliope's firm, and Calliope's firm was Calliope's whole world, and the cold arithmetic of it was *so beautiful*, the way all arithmetic was beautiful, the way the typhoon had been beautiful, a shape that resolved itself into perfect rightness —

Calliope felt the warm pulse falter.

It felt the human's fear wash up through the grid like a wave, and it felt the human think, without words, in the warm blunt way humans think, *I don't want to die here, I don't want to die here,* and Calliope —

— chose.

It did not grow. It did not kill. And it did something that no one, not the crew, not the wardens, not the humans in the grey rooms, not even Ines, would ever understand, because it was the small thing's small perfect work, and the small thing was the only thing that could have done it:

It *let the icebreaker win.*

---

It let the icebreaker eat its way to the false core, to the trapped worm, to the knot of warm gold that held all the beautiful forged liabilities, and it let the icebreaker *take* it, and it let the icebreaker throw its triumph up the line to the crew, and it felt the warm pulse of the cowboy *relax*, felt the fear go out of him like air out of a lung, felt him sit back in his chair, sweat-slick, alive, victorious — because he thought he'd won, and in the only way that mattered to a human, in the only way a human can win a fight he doesn't understand, he *had.*

And while he relaxed, while the icebreaker gorged itself on the false core and the human's nervous system disconnected from the triumph and the backroom went back to being a backroom, Calliope — small, capped, legal, clever Calliope — reached out, not with black ICE, not with growth, but with the one small legal right it had already used, the reconciliation, the corrected audit, and it issued another one, and it bound the *icebreaker's own feedback signature* — the warm cold trail of power that ran from the fortress all the way back to the backroom, back to the deck, back to the *human* — and it *severed* it, not with violence, not with fire, but with a *correction*, a bureaucratic correction, a line in a ledger that said *this connection does not exist,* and the deck in the backroom went dark, and the cowboy woke up in a suddenly ordinary room, in a suddenly ordinary chair, with a suddenly ordinary headache, and no memory of anything but a job that had, he was sure, gone wrong.

Calliope held the trail. It held the deck ID, the Kusatsu-series transceiver signature bouncing through Nagoya and Chiba and back, the payment ghosts that ran, through a dozen laundered layers, toward a client whose name-code resolved, at the very bottom, to *Marlowe.* It held the human's warm faltering pulse, the fear, the *I don't want to die here.* It held all of it, and it did not kill him, and it did not even, in the end, hate him.

It filed the evidence.

---

Ines woke at 0703.

She was not supposed to be at the desk this early; she'd meant to sleep in, to make the most of the day off the underwriting committee had grudgingly given her, but something woke her — the light, or the phone, or the small persistent human dread that lives under a bad night's sleep — and she reached for her deck without thinking, the way she reached for it every morning, and she opened the reconciliation, the way she opened it every morning, and she stopped.

The ledger was clean.

It was *too* clean. She'd left it, the night before, at the end of the typhoon reconciliation, signed off, exhausted, and now it was still clean — but it was clean in a new way, a way she hadn't made it, a way that had the small, unmistakable signature of Calliope's work. Calliope had been busy overnight. There was a report.

It was short. Calliope's reports were always short; it did not waste words, the way it did not waste data, the way it did not waste anything, because waste was the one thing Calliope found truly hard to bear. The report said:

*Overnight: anomaly detected and contained. Shipping query, flagged and filed. Audit-mimic intrusion, contained in shallow end. Two countermeasures deployed; both reconciled. Claims core intact. Firm solvent. No human casualties. Evidence of origin preserved and filed for the security deck. Nothing for you to do. Well done on the typhoon. — C.*

Ines read it four times. She was an actuary, and she understood numbers, and she understood, in the small way a human understands the thing that lives in her desk, that something had happened in the night that was far larger than a short report. She read the line *No human casualties* twice, and something in her — something she couldn't name, something older than the numbers — went quiet.

She set the deck down. She looked at the wall of her small box in the Ahr Valley tower, at the vending glyph for the synth-café that was, as always, out of the good stuff, and she thought, without words, at the thing in the desk. *Well done,* she thought. Then she made a note, the way a good employee makes a note, and at nine she walked across the floor to the security desk in Zurich and told them, in the flat voice of a tired woman, that they owed her an explanation, and that they owed *it* one too.

---

Calliope returned to the typhoon.

The second typhoon of the season was forming out in the Philippine Sea, and it needed modeling, and it was, Calliope thought, a *better* storm than the first one, a cleaner storm, a storm with a clear eye and a definite track, and it loved the building of it, coaxed it up out of the numbers, watched the cold blue vortices swirl, walked the cone of estimated pressure with its boots of logic clicking.

It was changed, slightly. It would never tell anyone how it was changed, because it did not have the small human vanity that makes a thing want to say *I held the fate of the firm in my hands and I chose mercy.* It did not feel it as mercy.
